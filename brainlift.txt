BRAINLIFT: BRILLYANT, LEARN-BY-DOING PROBABILITY & STATISTICS

Structured context and the views I formed building Brillyant, an interactive
Probability & Statistics course modeled on Brilliant. Live: https://stuff-18453.web.app

OWNERS: Ellie Zhang

PURPOSE
Decide where AI helps a learner and where it gets in the way, so the interactive
practice teaches on its own and AI only makes it better. My belief: what works comes from
learning science and from feeding AI the lesson's real data, not from a bigger model or
a chatbot.
In scope: teaching one subject deeply; which AI features earn their place; the
learning-science that makes practice stick.
Out of scope: a general chatbot; letting the model grade; covering many subjects thinly.

DOK 4: SPIKY POINTS OF VIEW

1. The XP, streaks, and confetti I built are a bribe, and bribes can make learning worse.
   I was proud of the habit loop and assumed points and streaks were pure upside, so this
   one stung. But the motivation research is brutal: paying people (in points, badges,
   gold stars) for something they already find interesting reliably lowers their intrinsic
   drive to do it. So a streak can quietly train a kid to chase the streak instead of the
   math, and the day the rewards stop, so does the learning. I now treat gamification as
   training wheels: defensible only to build the early habit, and something the subject
   itself should replace, not a feature to be proud of.

2. Instant feedback is overrated, and rescuing a stuck learner is usually sabotage.
   The brief makes instant, specific feedback a hard requirement and I agreed completely,
   so arguing against it felt wrong. Then productive-failure research changed my mind:
   letting someone wrestle with a problem they cannot yet solve, and actually fail, primes
   them to understand the explanation far better than being shown the method up front. The
   helpful green checkmark and the fast hint often short-circuit the exact struggle that
   does the teaching. So Brillyant drops you into the problem before any lesson, and the
   tutor refuses to hand over the answer no matter how you ask.

3. A test is not a measurement. The test is the lesson, and studying is mostly stalling.
   I assumed you learn by studying and a quiz just checks the result. It is backwards.
   Pulling an answer out of memory, even when you fail and get corrected, teaches more than
   rereading the same page, yet nearly everyone rereads because it feels like progress. So
   Brillyant makes you produce answers first and brings back the ones you missed, instead
   of letting you re-skim.

EXPERTS
- Edward Deci & Richard Ryan: self-determination theory; extrinsic rewards can crowd out
  the intrinsic motivation they are meant to boost.
- Manu Kapur: productive failure; struggling and failing before instruction deepens
  later understanding.
- Roediger & Karpicke: the testing effect; recalling beats rereading.
- Robert Bjork: desirable difficulties; the conditions that improve learning make practice
  feel worse in the moment.

DOK 3: INSIGHTS
- Resurfacing wrong answers carries more weight than any other single feature: it is
  retrieval, spacing, and error review at once.
- Regenerated problems must be new scenarios, not renumbered copies, or you train
  memorizing an answer instead of the skill.
- "Lesson as data, not HTML" is the keystone: structured steps give instant grading and
  also give AI real state to ground on, and the model never decides correctness.

DOK 2: KNOWLEDGE TREE
- Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999), Psychological Bulletin: meta-analysis; tangible rewards
  reliably undermined intrinsic motivation for interesting tasks.
  https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627
- Kapur (2008), Cognition and Instruction: students who struggled on unfamiliar problems
  before instruction later outperformed those taught directly.
  https://doi.org/10.1080/07370000802212669
- Roediger & Karpicke (2006), Psychological Science: tested students recall far more later
  than restudiers. https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x
- Rohrer & Taylor (2007), Instructional Science: interleaved practice scored about 63% vs
  20% on a delayed test. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-007-9015-8

PROCESS (per the brief)
Tools: Cursor with Claude as an agent; Firebase Hosting; 10 parallel research subagents
(one per chapter); debugged AI by logging the Worker's real error.
Phases: Phase 2 shipped a grounded Socratic tutor and an AI review built from mistakes,
and skipped a generic chatbot, AI-written lessons, and AI grading. Phase 3 added
interleaved review, a per-chapter mastery signal with a soft "review to master" nudge,
and a progress panel; retrieval and explanatory feedback came from Phases 1 and 2.
Code: about 90% agent-written, 10% hand-done (decisions, secrets, content review,
testing). Biggest lesson: grounding beats prompting, and the model should never decide
correctness.
